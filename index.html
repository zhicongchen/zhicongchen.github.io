I use computational methods to study the diffusion, use, and social impacts of emerging technologies, from anonymity networks to AI systems, to inform how they are governed.

I currently work as a Postdoctoral Fellow in the Department of Communications and New Media (CNM) at the National University of Singapore (NUS). Before joining NUS, I served as an Assistant Professor at the School of Journalism and Communication, Nanjing University (2022–2025).

I earned my Ph.D. in Communication from the Department of Media and Communication (COM), City University of Hong Kong (CityUHK) in 2022. There, I received rigorous and interdisciplinary training at the Web Mining Lab—a pioneering research hub founded and led by Prof. Jonathan J. H. Zhu (ICA Fellow, 2018) that has played a key role in establishing the field of computational communication research across the region since 2016.

01 / Research

A research program on emerging technologies and their governance.

03 / Teaching

Teaching people to reason with data and work with AI.

My courses combine computational practice, substantive communication questions, critical interpretation, research ethics, and reproducible workflows.

Computation becomes meaningful when students question its assumptions.

I design courses that move from code comprehension to analytical judgment: students learn how data are produced, how models encode choices, how evidence should be communicated, and where computational conclusions can fail.

Undergraduate + Graduate course levels
300+ students taught across undergraduate and graduate courses
3 top universities across Asia
4 years independent instructor
R + Python programming ecosystems
Teaching philosophy
  • Teach less, learn more. I talk less so students do more. Class time goes to live coding, debugging, and defending choices, not to covering every slide. Many students write their first line of code in my class, and they learn it by doing, not by watching.
  • A small core, mastered. Instead of surveying every package and method, students master a few workflows deeply enough to teach themselves the rest. Templates and annotated notebooks lower the barrier; optional extensions stretch advanced learners.
  • Fewer measures, stronger arguments. One validated metric beats ten convenient ones. Students defend each measure against alternative explanations (an engagement count may signal attention, endorsement, or outrage) and ask who is missing from their data and whether a finding would hold elsewhere.
  • Less automation, more judgment. Students first sketch an analysis on their own, then ask an AI assistant to attempt the same task, and compare the two: what the AI did better, what it missed, and what only they can judge.
