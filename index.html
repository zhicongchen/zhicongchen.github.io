Zhicong CHEN

I employ computational social science approaches to examine how and why communication infrastructures (re)shape knowledge, culture, and risks
across digital platforms and emerging technologies, through a longitudinal and historical perspective.

I currently work as a Postdoctoral Teaching Fellow in the Department of Communications and New Media (CNM) at the National University of Singapore (NUS). Before joining NUS, I served as an Assistant Professor at School of Journalism and Communication, Nanjing University (2022-2025).
I earned my Ph.D. in Communication from Department of Media and Communication (COM), City University of Hong Kong (CityUHK) in 2022, where I received rigorous and interdisplinary training at the Web Mining Lab , a pioneering research hub founded and led by Prof. Jonathan J. H. Zhu
(ICA Fellow, 2018) , and a key role in establishing the field of computational communication research across the region since 2016.

01 / Research interests

Research interests shaped by a common concern with communication infrastructure.

My work connects communication theory with computational methods to investigate how infrastructures enable participation, constrain visibility, reorganize social relationships, and shape collective understandings over time.

Anonymity, privacy, and online risk/safety

How do anonymous and pseudonymous infrastructures change disclosure, trust, misinformation, social support, and user sustainability across dark-web and surface-web communities?

Tor Dark web Online communities Cybersecurity

Platforms, behavior, and digital risk and resilience

How do platform architectures and communication environments influence behavior, emotion, exposure, deception, and public sense-making in political and health contexts?

Social media Misinformation Health communication Political communication

Cultural change through text as data

How can multilingual and diachronic corpora reveal long-term shifts in cultural values, geopolitical narratives, ideology, and the meanings attached to social categories?

Cultural analytics Semantic change Multilingual NLP Digital humanities

Computational Methods

  • Natural language processing
  • Network and cascade analysis
  • Machine learning
  • Survival and panel models
  • Big data analytics
  • Multilevel analysis

03 / Teaching

Teaching people to reason with data and work with AI.

My courses combine computational practice, substantive communication questions, critical interpretation, research ethics, and reproducible workflows.

Computation becomes meaningful when students question its assumptions.

I design courses that move from code comprehension to analytical judgment: students learn how data are produced, how models encode choices, how evidence should be communicated, and where computational conclusions can fail.

Undergraduate + Graduate course levels
300+ students taught across undergraduate and graduate courses
3 top universities across Asia
4 years independent instructor
R + Python programming ecosystems
Teaching philosophy
  • Lowering barriers without lowering standards. Many students write their first line of code in my class. Live coding, annotated notebooks, templates, and small-group troubleshooting build confidence, while optional extensions stretch advanced learners.
  • Every measurement is an argument. Each metric must be tied to a human validation process and defended against alternative explanations. An engagement count may signal attention, endorsement, or outrage; a network tie may signal agreement, opposition, or surveillance.
  • Context shapes the interpretations of data. Platform, language, population, and period shape what a finding means and where it travels. Students ask who is missing from their data and whether a conclusion would hold elsewhere, especially when tools built on English-language data meet other languages.
  • Knowing, learning from AI, and going beyond it. Students first sketch an analysis on their own, then ask an AI assistant to attempt the same task, and compare the two: what the AI did better, what it missed, and what only they can judge.
